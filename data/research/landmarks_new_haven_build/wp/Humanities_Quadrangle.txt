The '''Humanities Quadrangle''' ('''HQ'''), originally the '''Hall of Graduate Studies''' ('''HGS'''), is an academic [[Quadrangle (architecture)|quadrangle]] at [[Yale University]] in [[New Haven, Connecticut]]. First opened in 1932, the building was designed as a [[Collegiate Gothic]] structure by architect [[James Gamble Rogers]]. After serving for 86 years as the home of faculty offices and graduate student housing for the [[Yale Graduate School of Arts and Sciences]], the Hall of Graduate Studies underwent a series of major renovations designed by [http://www.annumarchitects.com Annum Architects] (formerly Ann Beha Architects)<ref>{{Cite web |last=Gonzalez |first=Susan |date=2021-04-01 |title=Humanities Quadrangle: A cherished Yale icon reimagined |url=https://news.yale.edu/2021/04/01/humanities-quadrangle-cherished-yale-icon-reimagined |access-date=2024-09-12 |website=YaleNews |language=en}}</ref> starting in 2018.

It reopened as the Humanities Quadrangle in 2021, now serving as the home of 15 academic departments and several humanities programs. With 311 offices, 28 classrooms, and 24 meeting spaces, the Humanities Quadrangle is frequently used by students and faculty.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |last=Gonzalez |first=Susan |date=2021-04-01 |title=Humanities Quadrangle: A cherished Yale icon reimagined |url=https://news.yale.edu/2021/04/01/humanities-quadrangle-cherished-yale-icon-reimagined |access-date=2023-06-06 |website=YaleNews |language=en}}</ref>  Among others, HQ houses [[Yale College]]'s Directed Studies program and the Whitney Humanities Center.

== History ==

=== Before academic use ===
Located at 320 York Street in [[New Haven, Connecticut]],<ref name=":0" /> the site of the Humanities Quadrangle has undergone significant changes throughout its past. A map from 1824 shows the area as largely undeveloped, aside from a few small buildings along the curb of York Street.<ref>{{cite map|author=Amos Doolittle|title=Plan of New Haven|date=1824-05-28|url=https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/15675074|scale=[ca. 1:3,750]|language=en|access-date=2023-06-06}}</ref> By 1852, part of the land belonged to the local [[Christ Church (New Haven)|Christ Church]],<ref>{{cite map|author=R. Whiteford|title=Map of the city of New Haven|year=1852|url=https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/15675092|scale=[ca. 1:15,840]|publisher=L.S. Punderson|language=en|access-date=2023-06-06}}</ref> and ownership was transferred to the [[New Haven Wheel Company]] by 1886.<ref>{{cite map|author=Sanborn Map & Publishing Co.|title=Insurance maps of New Haven, Connecticut. Volume 2.|year=1886|url=https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/11175977|scale=1:600|page=30|language=en|access-date=2023-06-06}}</ref> The company vacated its premises by 1901,<ref>{{cite map|author=Sanborn-Perris Map Co.|title=Insurance maps of New Haven, Connecticut. Volume 1.|year=1901|url=https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/11188890|scale=1:600|page=48|language=en|access-date=2023-06-06}}</ref> and buildings adjacent to the former wheel factory became [[North American fraternity and sorority housing|fraternity housing]] by 1924.<ref>{{cite map|author=Sanborn Map Company|title=Insurance maps of New Haven, Connecticut. Volume 2.|year=1924|url=https://collections.library.yale.edu/catalog/11190119|scale=1:600|page=206|language=en|access-date=2023-06-06}}</ref>

=== Original construction ===
In a February 1929 speech to recent graduates, [[Yale University]] President [[James Rowland Angell|James Angell]] announced plans for building a new [[Quadrangle (architecture)|quadrangle]] to house the [[Yale Graduate School of Arts and Sciences|Yale Graduate School]],<ref>{{Cite news |title=$4,000,000 ASSURED FOR YALE BUILDINGS; President Angell Tells Alumni Sterling Estate Will Provide $2,000,000 for Dormitories. LIKE SUM FOR GYMNASIUM Unnamed Source Promises These Funds--New Structure Will Be on Another Site. HOUSING PLANS EXTENSIVE Program Akin to Oxford and Cambridge Method--Faculty SalaryIncreases Recited to Graduates. Graduates Visit Classrooms. Gymnasium a "Winter Playground." Tells of Better Faculty Salaries. |language=en |work=The New York Times |url=http://timesmachine.nytimes.comhttp//arch-timesmachine-fe-prd-40741-2-575473780.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com/timesmachine/1929/02/23/95737250.html?pageNumber=12 |access-date=2023-06-18}}</ref> and construction began the next year.<ref name=":022">{{Cite web |title=History of Yale Graduate School {{!}} Yale Graduate School of Arts & Sciences |url=https://gsas.yale.edu/deans-office/history-yale-graduate-school |access-date=2023-06-18 |website=gsas.yale.edu |archive-date=2021-08-17 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210817115425/https://gsas.yale.edu/deans-office/history-yale-graduate-school |url-status=dead }}</ref> Some had expected this development to cause the demolition of [[Mory's]], but blueprints were drawn to exclude the historic restaurant.<ref>{{Cite news |title=FOR YALE GRADUATE SCHOOL; Plans Drawn Do Not Include Mory's Association Building. |language=en |work=The New York Times |url=http://timesmachine.nytimes.comhttp//timesmachine.content-tagging.us-east-1-01.prd.dvsp.nyt.net/timesmachine/1930/04/16/96098028.html?pageNumber=4 |access-date=2023-06-18}}</ref> Although $3.5 million was initially budgeted, the final cost was only $2.5 million, which was financed by funds from the estate of [[John William Sterling|John Sterling]].<ref>{{Cite news |title=YALE BUILDING OUTLAY PUT AT $28,000,000; Value of Structures Under Way Totals $20,000,000--Some to Be Ready This Fall. |language=en |work=The New York Times |url=http://timesmachine.nytimes.comhttp//arch-timesmachine-fe-prd-40741-2-575473780.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com/timesmachine/1930/08/24/118186375.html?pageNumber=49 |access-date=2023-06-18}}</ref><ref name=":12">{{Cite news |title=YALE BUILDINGS READY.; Seven Structures Are Completed for Opening of New Term. |language=en |work=The New York Times |url=http://timesmachine.nytimes.comhttp//arch-timesmachine-fe-prd-40741-2-575473780.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com/timesmachine/1932/08/29/100846128.html?pageNumber=13 |access-date=2023-06-19}}</ref> The building was officially named after its benefactor,<ref name=":12" /> but the Sterling surname was dropped over time, leading to the Hall of Graduate Studies.<ref name=":022" />

The Hall of Graduate Studies opened its doors for the first time on September 29, 1932, coinciding with the start of the academic year. The new quadrangle included 80 offices, 16 classrooms, a 200-foot tower, and [[Dormitory|dormitories]] for 225 students.<ref>{{Cite news |title=YALE OPENS THURSDAY; NEW BUILDINGS READY; Gymnasium, Divinity Quadrangle and a Sheffield Addition Finished -- Faculty Changes Made. |language=en |work=The New York Times |url=http://timesmachine.nytimes.comhttp//arch-timesmachine-fe-prd-40741-2-575473780.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com/timesmachine/1932/09/26/100854881.html?pageNumber=12 |access-date=2023-06-20}}</ref>

=== Modern renovations ===
[[File:Swensen Tower, Yale Humanities Quadrangle.jpg|thumb|right|upright=.65|[[David F. Swensen|Swensen]] Tower]]
In March 2015, Yale received a $25 million donation towards transforming the Hall of Graduate Studies into a center for the [[humanities]], which prompted the building's iconic 14-story tower to be named after [[David F. Swensen|David Swensen]].<ref name=":0" /><ref>{{Cite web |date=2015-03-25 |title=Gift to transform Hall of Graduate Studies honors David Swensen |url=https://news.yale.edu/2015/03/25/gift-transform-hall-graduate-studies-honors-david-swensen |access-date=2023-06-20 |website=YaleNews |language=en}}</ref> In addition, another $50 million was provided by an anonymous donor in January 2016,<ref>{{Cite web |date=2016-01-19 |title=$50 million gift to help fund humanities hub, create endowment |url=https://news.yale.edu/2016/01/19/50-million-gift-help-fund-humanities-hub-create-endowment |access-date=2023-06-20 |website=YaleNews |language=en}}</ref> contributing to a total budget of $162 million.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Connolly |first=Bess |date=2019-08-19 |title=New Yale humanities hub honored for its excellence in planning |url=https://news.yale.edu/2019/08/19/new-yale-humanities-hub-honored-its-excellence-planning |access-date=2023-06-20 |website=YaleNews |language=en}}</ref> By September 2017, the city's [[Board of Alderman|Board of Alders]] approved the new site plans,<ref>{{Cite web |date=2017-09-19 |title=Grad Housing Conversion Gets Alder OK |url=https://www.newhavenindependent.org/article/hgs_conversion_ |access-date=2023-06-20 |website=New Haven Independent |language=en}}</ref><ref name=":1">{{Cite report |url=https://www.newhavenct.gov/home/showpublisheddocument/13610/637883172708970000 |title=New Haven City Plan Commission Site Plan Review |last=Mattison |first=Edward |date=2017-09-19 |access-date=2023-07-02}}</ref> and construction began in August 2018.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Feibel |first=Adelaide |date=2018-08-30 |title=Construction begins on HGS renovations |url=https://yaledailynews.com/blog/2018/08/30/construction-begins-on-hgs-renovations/ |access-date=2023-06-20 |website=Yale Daily News |language=en}}</ref>

The renovations were originally slated to be done by 2020, but delays due to the [[COVID-19 pandemic]] caused the project to finish a year behind schedule. The building officially reopened on February 15, 2021 as the Humanities Quadrangle, although some facilities were not completed until May 2021.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Hahamy |first=Madison |date=2021-03-11 |title=Humanities Quadrangle opens its doors — to some |url=https://yaledailynews.com/blog/2021/03/11/humanities-quadrangle-opens-its-doors-to-some/ |access-date=2023-06-20 |website=Yale Daily News |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Decker |first=Maia |date=2021-05-04 |title=Humanities Quadrangle serves as new home to 15 departments |url=https://yaledailynews.com/blog/2021/05/04/humanities-quadrangle-serves-as-new-home-to-15-departments/ |access-date=2023-06-20 |website=Yale Daily News |language=en}}</ref> About 13,000 square feet of underground space was added during renovations, resulting in a total floor area of 173,811 square feet.<ref name=":0" /><ref name=":1" />

== Architecture ==

=== Finnegan's contributions ===
During construction of the Hall of Graduate Studies, a man known only as Mr. Finnegan was placed in charge of building operations. Finnegan was tasked with executing [[James Gamble Rogers]]'s plans, but he made modifications to express a personal disdain for the self-importance of graduate schools.<ref name=":2">{{Cite book |last=Furniss |first=Edgar Stevenson |title=The Graduate School of Yale: A Brief History |publisher=Carl Purington Rollins Printing-Office |year=1965 |pages=69–72 |language=en |asin=B0007DU7RG}}</ref>
[[File:Yale Humanities Quadrangle Angel Brick.jpg|thumb|One of Finnegan's special bricks in the Yale Humanities Quadrangle, with an angel carving.]]
The first of the unauthorized changes was the use of bricks with unique carvings. Although [[Yale University|Yale]] had requested bricks with its name emblazoned on the surface, Finnegan created additional designs to represent senior university officials. These included an angel ([[James Rowland Angell|James Angell]]), a Maltese cross ([[Wilbur Lucius Cross|Wilbur Cross]]), and a furnace ([[Edgar S. Furniss|Edgar Furniss]]).<ref name=":2" /> Many of the special bricks can still be seen in the building's lobby.

Finnegan also directed the installation of lifelike busts of Rogers and Thomas Farnam, the university's [[comptroller]].<ref>{{Cite news |title=THOMAS W. FARNAM; Former Yale Official, a Leader of Red Cross in World War |language=en |work=The New York Times |url=http://timesmachine.nytimes.comhttp//arch-timesmachine-fe-prd-40741-2-575473780.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com/timesmachine/1943/08/04/83933644.html?pageNumber=17 |access-date=2023-07-03}}</ref> The busts were displayed prominently within the building's [[portico]], and they were discovered by Farnam himself during an inspection visit. Farnam demanded for the sculptures to be removed, but workers were only able to whittle them down into the resemblance of Egyptian [[pharaoh]]s.<ref name=":2" />

After the Hall of Graduate Studies opened, a previously unnoticed inscription was found on the archway of the building's entrance. The text was from the novel [[Scaramouche (novel)|Scaramouche]] by [[Rafael Sabatini]], stating that "He was born with a gift for laughter and a sense that the world was mad." University officials were upset by the inscription's non-scholarly origin, and after a brief investigation, it was revealed that Finnegan was responsible for the unsanctioned chiseling. However, Rogers would go on to claim that the text was proposed by a committee of Yale faculty.<ref name=":2" /><ref>{{Cite news |title=HERETICAL CARVING AT YALE IS TRACED; Faculty Itself Chose Excerpt From Best-Seller to Grace Portal, Architect Says. NEVER LOOKED AT, ANYWAY Inscriptions Chosen Chiefly if They Fit in Space and Cast Good Shadows, He Explains. |language=en |work=The New York Times |url=http://timesmachine.nytimes.comhttp//arch-timesmachine-fe-prd-40741-2-575473780.us-east-1.elb.amazonaws.com/timesmachine/1934/11/17/95496363.html?pageNumber=17 |access-date=2023-07-03}}</ref>

=== Entryway stone heads ===
The archway above the entrance of the building also features 12 stone heads, which face incoming foot traffic from the street. They represent a selection of the 85 [[Drafter|draftsmen]] who worked on the construction of the Hall of Graduate Studies, but only 11 surnames have been associated with the carvings. Unlike the adjacent textual inscription by Finnegan, the stone heads were approved in advance by the site's architects.<ref>{{Cite web |title=THE STONE HEADS OF 320 YORK {{!}} Whitney Humanities Center |url=https://whc.yale.edu/stone-heads-320-york |access-date=2023-07-04 |website=whc.yale.edu}}</ref>

== Other ==
In May 2018, the building was the site of an incident where a Black Yale graduate student was reported to the police for napping in her dorm's common room. The early morning encounter was streamed on [[Facebook live|Facebook Live]] and sparked controversy over [[racial profiling]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Griggs |first=Brandon |date=2018-05-09 |title=A black Yale graduate student took a nap in her dorm's common room.
