[[File:Rudolph Hall.png|thumb|upright=1.2|Rudolph Hall in 2022, showing the 2008 addition to the right of Paul Rudolph's original Brutalist structure]]
'''Rudolph Hall''' (built as the '''Yale Art and Architecture Building,''' nicknamed the '''A & A Building''', and given its present name in 2007<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |date=2014-05-06 |title=Yale's Rudolph Hall receives Preservation Trust's highest honor |url=https://news.yale.edu/2014/05/06/yale-s-rudolph-hall-receives-preservation-trust-s-highest-honor |access-date=2023-02-02 |website=YaleNews |language=en}}</ref>) is one of the earliest and best-known examples of [[Brutalist architecture]] in the United States. Completed in 1963 in [[New Haven]], [[Connecticut]], the building houses [[Yale University]]'s [[Yale School of Architecture|School of Architecture]]. Until 2000, it also housed the [[Yale School of Art|School of Art]].

== Construction ==
Designed by the building's namesake, [[architect]] [[Paul Rudolph (architect)|Paul Rudolph]], the complex building contains more than 30 floor levels in its seven stories. The building is made of ribbed, [[Bush hammer|bush-hammered]] concrete. The design was influenced by [[Frank Lloyd Wright]]'s [[Larkin Administration Building]] in [[Buffalo, New York]], and the later buildings of [[Le Corbusier]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Mortice |first=Zach |date=January 18, 2008 |title=Gwathmey Helps Yale Architecture School Icon Re-emerge |url=https://www.aia.org/aiarchitect/thisweek08/0118/0118n_yale.cfm |access-date=2023-02-03 |website=AIA Architect|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080121093334/https://www.aia.org/aiarchitect/thisweek08/0118/0118n_yale.cfm |archive-date=2008-01-21 }}</ref>

== History ==
The building was dedicated on November 8, 1963,<ref name=":1">{{Cite web |last=Branch |first=Mark Alden |date=February 1998 |title=The Building That Won't Go Away |url=http://archives.yalealumnimagazine.com/issues/98_02/AA.html |access-date=2023-02-02 |website=Yale Alumni Magazine |archive-date=2015-11-28 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151128103512/http://archives.yalealumnimagazine.com/issues/98_02/AA.html |url-status=live }}</ref> to wide praise by critics and [[Academic staff|academics]]. It received several prestigious awards, including the ''Award of Honor'' by the [[American Institute of Architects]]. [[The New York Times|New York Times]] architecture [[critic]] [[Ada Louise Huxtable]] called it "a spectacular ''tour de force''."<ref name=Pogrebin>{{Citation  | last=Pogrebin  | first=Robin  | title=Renovating a Master's Shrine: Yale's Art and Architecture Building  | newspaper=[[The New York Times]]  | pages=  | date=July 1, 2006  | url=https://www.nytimes.com/2006/07/01/arts/design/01yale.html  | access-date=February 23, 2017  | archive-date=April 7, 2019  | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190407113200/https://www.nytimes.com/2006/07/01/arts/design/01yale.html  | url-status=live  }}</ref> But the building also had detractors from the start. In a speech at the dedication ceremony, architecture historian [[Nikolaus Pevsner]] bemoaned what he called the structure's oppressive monumentality.<ref name=":1" /><ref name="Times" /> 

Over the following decade, the critical reaction to the building became more negative.<ref name="Times">{{Citation  | last=Ourousoff  | first=Nicolai  | title=Yale Revelation: Renewal for a Building and Its Original Designer  | newspaper=[[The New York Times]]  | pages=E1  | date=August 28, 2008  | url=https://www.nytimes.com/2008/08/28/arts/design/28yale.html  | access-date=February 23, 2017  | archive-date=March 10, 2017  | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170310091859/http://www.nytimes.com/2008/08/28/arts/design/28yale.html  | url-status=live  }}</ref>

A large fire on the night of June 14, 1969, caused extensive damage. The fire was rumored to have been set by anti-establishment protesters, but this charge has remained unproven.<ref name="globe">{{cite news |last=Rohan |first=Timothy |date=September 7, 2014 |title=The dream behind Boston's forbidding Government Service Center |newspaper=Boston Globe |url=https://www.bostonglobe.com/ideas/2014/09/07/the-dream-behind-boston-forbidding-government-service-center/zmTu4rsTtcfTqQKjy8sbtO/story.html |access-date=February 4, 2022}}</ref> 

During the repairs, many changes were made to Rudolph's original design. Mezzanine levels were inserted, spaces broken up, and double-glazing added.<ref name=":2">{{Cite web |last=Mairs |first=Jessica |date=September 26, 2014 |title=Brutalism: Yale Art and Architecture Building by Paul Rudolph |url=https://www.dezeen.com/2014/09/26/yale-art-and-architecture-building-paul-rudolph-brutalism/ |access-date=2023-02-02 |website=Dezeen |language=en |archive-date=2023-02-02 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230202024708/https://www.dezeen.com/2014/09/26/yale-art-and-architecture-building-paul-rudolph-brutalism/ |url-status=live }}</ref> Later, a renovation scheme by [[Beyer Blinder Belle]] was commissioned but only partially executed, if at all.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2008-11-14 |title=Architectural Dialogue: A Conversation Between Dean Robert Stern and Architect Charles Gwathmey |url=https://news.yale.edu/2008/11/14/architectural-dialogue-conversation-between-dean-robert-stern-and-architect-charles-gwath |access-date=2023-02-02 |website=YaleNews |language=en |archive-date=2023-02-02 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230202050625/https://news.yale.edu/2008/11/14/architectural-dialogue-conversation-between-dean-robert-stern-and-architect-charles-gwath |url-status=live }}</ref>  

In 2000, the School of Art moved out to its own building.  

As the building approached its 40th year, appreciation of the structure had increased to the point that Yale began planning a renovation and addition. In 2001, initial commissions were given to [[David Childs]] of [[Skidmore, Owings & Merrill]] for the former and [[Richard Meier]] for the latter.<ref>{{Cite web |date=February 9, 2001 |title=Yale chooses architects for arts projects |url=http://archives.news.yale.edu/v29.n18/story2.html |access-date=2023-02-02 |website=Yale Bulletin and Calendar |archive-date=2023-02-02 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230202160245/http://archives.news.yale.edu/v29.n18/story2.html |url-status=live }}</ref> Then Yale "parted ways" with those two, and gave the commission to [[Gwathmey Siegel & Associates Architects]], whose [[Charles Gwathmey]] was a Yale Architecture [[alumnus]] and former student of Rudolph's.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Amelar |first=Sarah |date=February 19, 2009 |title=Paul Rudolph Hall and Jeffery H. Loria Center for the History of Art |url=https://www.architecturalrecord.com/articles/8154-paul-rudolph-hall-and-jeffery-h-loria-center-for-the-history-of-art |access-date=2023-02-02 |website=Architectural Record |language=en |archive-date=2023-02-02 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230202050624/https://www.architecturalrecord.com/articles/8154-paul-rudolph-hall-and-jeffery-h-loria-center-for-the-history-of-art |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="Times" /> Yale spent $126 million on the project between 2007 and 2008, including a $20 million gift for the purpose from alumnus [[Sid Bass]].<ref>{{Cite news |last=Needham |first=Paul |date=February 6, 2008 |title=A&A; Building renovation to restore historic elements |work=Yale Daily News |url=http://www.yaledailynews.com/articles/view/23354 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090113165836/http://www.yaledailynews.com/articles/view/23354 |access-date=February 3, 2023 |archive-date=January 13, 2009 |url-status=live }}</ref> The renovation returned the building to more closely hew to Rudolph's design.<ref name=":0" /><ref name="Times" /> At the same time, an addition was built with classroom and office space, two lecture theatres, a cafe, and a ground-floor library.<ref name=":2" /> 

In 2014, the building received the Landmark Plaque, the highest honor bestowed by the New Haven Preservation Trust.<ref name=":0" />
