The '''Hotel Marcel''' (formerly the '''Armstrong Rubber Company Building''' or the '''Pirelli Tire Building''') is located in the [[Long Wharf, New Haven|Long Wharf]] district of [[New Haven, Connecticut]], United States. The nine-story building was designed by [[modernist]] architect [[Marcel Breuer]] in the [[Brutalist architecture|Brutalist]] style and originally functioned as an office headquarters for the Armstrong Rubber Company. Since 2022, it has operated as a [[Hilton Worldwide|Hilton]] hotel with 165 rooms. Over the years, the building has received extensive commentary from architecture critics, particularly for its [[massing]] or shape. The building is listed on the Connecticut Register of Historic Places and the [[National Register of Historic Places]].

The Hotel Marcel's massing is composed of a two-story base, originally used for [[research and development]], and a five-story upper section, originally used as offices. The two sections are separated by a two-story air gap with three massive [[Pier (architecture)|piers]]. The facade is made of [[Mo-Sai]] concrete, with recessed windows and an entrance to the east. The base originally contained warehouses and research laboratories extending further west, although most of the extended base was demolished in 2003. The structural system is made of steel and concrete, with the upper floors suspended from a series of [[truss]]es. Inside, there is a ground-floor lobby and lounge, with hotel rooms above and an event space on the top floor. The modern hotel is a [[zero-energy building]], relying on solar panels. The building's site includes a freestanding concrete sign to the northeast.

The Armstrong Rubber Company acquired the site in 1966 and developed its headquarters there from 1968 to 1970. [[Pirelli]] acquired Armstrong Rubber in 1988 and moved out of the building in May 1999. For the next two decades, the building was predominantly vacant, being used only for occasional events such as art exhibitions. Following an unsuccessful attempt in 1999 to build a [[shopping mall]] on the site, the building was sold to [[Westfield Group]] and then to [[IKEA]], which demolished part of the base. The architect and developer Bruce Redman Becker bought the building in January 2020 and began converting it into a hotel that September. The hotel opened in May 2022.

[[File:Hotel Marcel, formerly Pirelli Tire parking.jpg|left|thumb|Hotel Marcel's freestanding concrete sign]]
At the northeastern corner of the site is a freestanding concrete sign measuring {{Convert|48|ft}} high and {{Convert|36|ft|m||adj=}} wide. Built at the same time as the main structure in 1968–1969, the sign is a contributing feature in the site's National Register of Historic Places listing.<ref name="NPS p. 7">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2021|ps=.|page=7}}</ref> The building's principal architect, [[Marcel Breuer]], designed the sign after rejecting an earlier proposal to place signage above the roof.<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> The sign consists of a central pedestal supporting a rectangular frame made up of two narrow vertical [[Pier (architecture)|piers]] flanking an open void.<ref name="NPS p. 7" /><ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> Above the frame is a concrete mass with square openings on its sides. The frame originally displayed the Armstrong Rubber name, and later owners altered it to show their own branding.<ref name="NPS p. 7" /> The pedestal contains a small east-facing door and a west-facing window, providing access to storage space inside.<ref name="NPS p. 7" /> The door was added to circumvent local height restrictions on signs; because of this feature, the city [[zoning]] code classified the structure as a building rather than a sign, exempting it from those limits.<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /><ref name="Gatje pp. 213–214">{{harvnb|Gatje|2000|ps=.|pages=213–214}}</ref><ref name="CHC p. 11">{{harvnb|Connecticut Historical Commission|2000|ps=.|page=11}}</ref> The Connecticut Historical Commission has compared the sign to a [[carillon]] that Breuer designed for the [[College of Saint Benedict and Saint John's University]] in Minnesota.<ref name="CHC p. 11" />

== Architecture ==
The Hotel Marcel was designed by modernist architects [[Marcel Breuer]] and [[Robert F. Gatje]] in the [[Brutalist architecture|Brutalist]] style.<ref name="Brown q486" /><ref name="Hyman y245">{{cite book |last=Hyman |first=Isabelle |title=Marcel Breuer, Architect: The Career and the Buildings |date=2001 |publisher=Harry N. Abrams |isbn=978-0-8109-4265-3 |page=269}}</ref> [[Paul Weidlinger]] and his associate Matthys Levy were the structural engineers.<ref name="NPS p. 5">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2021|ps=.|page=5}}</ref> Originally an office building, the Hotel Marcel structure was converted into a hotel from 2020 to 2022.<ref name="AR 2022" />

==== Facade ====
[[File:Pirelli Tire Building New Haven.jpg|thumb|Detail of the south elevation. The central bay is wider than the others, with one recessed window on the far eastern side of each story. The spandrels between each story are beveled. Atop the building, the mechanical story's concrete panels have reliefs, which conceal diagonal trusses.|alt=Refer to caption]]The facade's west and east [[Elevation (architecture)|elevations]] are divided into 36 [[Bay (architecture)|bays]], while the shorter north and south elevations are divided into 13 bays.<ref name="NPS pp. 5–6">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2021|ps=.|pages=5–6}}</ref> Each of the bays is generally {{Convert|5|ft}} wide.<ref name="AR 2022" /><ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> The exception is the central bay of the north and south elevations, which is {{Convert|24|ft}} wide<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> and is embedded into the piers supporting the building.<ref name="NPS pp. 5–6" /> The facade is made of concrete, like many of Breuer's other designs; during his career, he had been especially involved in using concrete as a decorative feature.<ref name="CHC p. 9">{{harvnb|Connecticut Historical Commission|2000|ps=.|page=9}}</ref> The concrete panels are made of [[Mo-Sai]] mixture<ref name="PHN p. 2">{{harvnb|Passive House Network|2023|ps=.|page=2}}</ref><ref name="p2771883629" /> which has horizontal and vertical accents.<ref name="NPS pp. 5–6" /> There are 525 concrete panels,<ref name="Arch 2" /> of which some were cast on-site during construction, while others were [[Precast concrete|precast]].<ref name="NPS pp. 5–6" /><ref name="p550191037" /> Parts of the panels have a rougher texture than the rest of the facade, as they were poured into wooden [[formwork]].<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="McCarter p. 384">{{harvnb|McCarter|2024|ps=.|page=384}}</ref> The panels are separated by deep [[Joint (building)|joints]]<ref name="NPS p. 6" /> and were coated with a layer of insulation during the 2020s hotel renovation.<ref name="p3191365872" /><ref name="Glusac w868">{{cite web |last=Glusac |first=Elaine |date=April 26, 2022 |title=Hotels With Sustainability and Net-Zero Ambitions |url-access=limited |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2022/04/26/travel/hotels-sustainability-net-zero.html |access-date=December 12, 2025 |website=The New York Times |archive-date=May 30, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250530143840/https://www.nytimes.com/2022/04/26/travel/hotels-sustainability-net-zero.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

The facade has 525 windows,<ref name="Vogue" /><ref name="Arch 2" /> which are recessed {{Convert|30|in}} from the concrete panels<ref name="p3191365872" /> and are set into immovable metal frames.<ref name="NPS p. 5" /> Each window opening measures {{Convert|3|by|6|ft}} across,<ref name="Tucker e785">{{cite web |last=Tucker |first=Ethan |date=March 30, 2023 |title=A Marcel Breuer Icon Gets a Second Life as a Sustainable Hotel |url=https://metropolismag.com/projects/marcel-breuer-hotel-new-haven/ |access-date=December 12, 2025 |website=Metropolis |archive-date=September 17, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250917115513/https://metropolismag.com/projects/marcel-breuer-hotel-new-haven/ |url-status=live }}</ref> surrounded by [[bevel]]ed concrete on all four sides.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> The bevels shield occupants from excessive sunlight and create a visual impression of depth,<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30">{{harvnb|Nieminen|2022|ps=.|page=30}}</ref><ref name="Docomomo" /> casting shadows of different shapes throughout the day.<ref name="NPS p. 5" /><ref name="PHN p. 2" /><ref name="p550191037" /> During the hotel conversion, the original windows were replaced with triple-glazed panes to reduce indoor temperature fluctuations<ref name="CNN" /><ref name="Arch 2" /><ref name="p3191365872" /> and to block highway noise.<ref name="AR 2022" /><ref name="Webb f221">{{cite web |last=Webb |first=Michael |date=September 1, 2022 |title=Repurposing a Brutalist Icon |url=https://www.theplan.it/eng/interior-design-contract/005/repurposing-a-brutalist-icon |access-date=December 12, 2025 |website=theplan.it |archive-date=December 6, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251206211130/https://www.theplan.it/eng/interior-design-contract/005/repurposing-a-brutalist-icon |url-status=live }}</ref>

The main entrance is located within the eight center bays of the eastern elevation. The entryway has granite floor tiles, a protruding concrete [[Canopy (architecture)|canopy]], and a [[soffit]] or ceiling made of [[cement plaster]].<ref name="NPS p. 5" /> It contains a central vestibule with angled concrete walls, which converge at a pair of double doors topped by a transom window; this vestibule is flanked by recessed windows.<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /><ref name="NPS p. 5" /> On the north and south elevations, the central bay has one recessed window at the far eastern end of each story, alternating with beveled [[spandrel]] panels between the different stories.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> In the space between the upper and lower stories, the central bay is clad with rough-textured concrete panels, which are staggered vertically.<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> A stair from the north, and a ramp from the south, lead to additional utility doors on these elevations.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /> The remainder of the piers are clad in cast concrete.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" />

The double-height mechanical space on the ninth story is windowless, with concrete panels measuring two bays wide.<ref name="NPS p. 5" /><ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> The mechanical story's panels have reliefs, some of which conceal the building's [[truss]]work.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 31" /> The panels on the west and east elevations are beveled at their bottoms and tops. On the north and south elevations, the trusswork cuts diagonally across the beveled panels.<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> The demolished portion of the research laboratories had been clad in the same material as the main building. The warehouse portion had contained {{Convert|10|ft|m|-wide|adj=mid}} precast-concrete panels, with a central window and beveled edges at the top and bottom of each panel.<ref name="McCarter pp. 383–384">{{harvnb|McCarter|2024|ps=.|pages=383–384}}</ref>

=== Structural and mechanical features ===
The foundation is composed of 758 [[deep foundation|deep pilings]], since the building is constructed on former [[mudflat]]s.<ref name="Brown q486" /> The structural system of the main building consists of [[corrugated metal]] ceilings, concrete floors and walls, and steel I-beam columns.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> At ground level, the cast-concrete floor is embedded into the foundation. These structural systems were also used in the research-laboratory portion of the demolished annex, while the warehouse portion had column-free spaces covered by precast concrete roof beams.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> The building was one of the first in the U.S. to have floor plates suspended from overhead [[cantilevered]] [[trusses]].<ref name="1969 AF" /><ref name="p550191037" /> Each of the upper floors is supported by seven {{Convert|50|ST|LT t|adj=on}} trusses.<ref name="AR 2022" /><ref name="1969 AF" /> The exterior curtain wall is also suspended from the trusses.<ref name="Gatje p. 189">{{harvnb|Gatje|2000|ps=.|page=189}}</ref> In addition to the three large piers connecting the building's lower and upper sections,<ref name="NPS p. 5" /> there are two parallel sets of recessed columns between these piers.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" />

===Interior===
The building was constructed as the headquarters for Armstrong Rubber Company.<ref name="1969 AF" /> The building originally had {{convert|183000|sqft}};<ref name="1969 AF" /> excluding demolished portions, the surviving structure is estimated at {{cvt|107100|sqft}}.<ref name="AR 2022" /> The lowest two stories contained laboratories and were used for [[research and development]], and the demolished section of the building was used as a warehouse and production facility.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="Docomomo" /> Three sides of the base were taken up by research laboratories, while tire-testing labs with high ceilings were located in the warehouse.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> Originally, the upper section was used as offices.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="Docomomo" />  In general, the interiors of both the main building and its demolished annex are arranged on a grid of square modules measuring {{Convert|30|ft}} on each side. The main building is divided into six north–south and three west–east modules; the center bay of the west–east dimension is only {{Convert|24|ft}} wide, corresponding to the piers' width.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> The modules are further divided into sections measuring {{Convert|5|ft}} wide, corresponding with the facade's bays.<ref name="AR 2022" />

The central pier has elevators, accessed by elevator lobbies with granite floors.<ref name="NPS p. 7" /> The main stairs are located within the piers at the building's north and south ends. The staircases connect all of the building's floors, and the northern staircase extends to the roof.<ref name="NPS p. 7" /> The stairs have concrete [[Stringer (stairs)|stringer]] boards and walls, terrazzo steps, and wooden handrails.<ref name="Stephens y349" /><ref name="NPS p. 7" /> The stairs are similar to a set that Breuer had designed for the [[Whitney Museum]]'s building at [[945 Madison Avenue]].<ref name="Stephens y349" /> The landings of each stair have terrazzo floors, concrete walls, and metal louvers beneath the windows in the landings.<ref name="NPS p. 7" /> After the hotel's completion, the elevators were retrofitted with [[regenerative braking]] equipment.<ref name="Kuehner-Hebert x146" /><ref name="Shilling n140" />

Many of the original decorative finishes were removed in the 2000s.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /> As part of the hotel's development, the interior was redesigned by Dutch East Design,<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /><ref name="Stephens y349" /> which added hotel rooms, a restaurant, a bar, and an event space.<ref name="AR 2022" /><ref name="USHist">{{Cite web |last=Leasca |first=Stacey |date=December 15, ==== Other stories ====
Before the hotel renovation, the outer portions of each story had individual offices.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> The inner portions were used as conference and meeting spaces,<ref name="NPS p. 6" /> and they also contained [[open plan]] work areas for assistants.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /> The modern-day building includes 165 guest rooms,<ref name="AR 2022" /><ref name="p2771883629">{{Cite magazine |last=Marsh |first=Don |date=May 2022 |title=Mo-Sai: Brutally resilient |magazine=Concrete Products |page=56 |volume=75 |issue=5 |id={{ProQuest|2771883629}}}}</ref> placed within the second and the fifth to eighth floors.<ref name="Breen 2020" /> The hotel rooms have a subdued color scheme<ref name="Zeitoun e204" /> consisting of contrasting grays and walnut wood.<ref name="Vogue" /><ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 31" /> The walls are made of off-white vinyl panels interspersed with lighting and dark-green or sienna wood decorations.<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 31" /> They feature [[Cesca chair]]s designed by Marcel Breuer, as well as custom modular furniture for storage designed by Dutch East.<ref name="Vogue" /> The furniture by Dutch East, designed with [[Bauhaus]] motifs, interlocks with each other<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 31" /> and was largely manufactured in the surrounding region.<ref name="St John t211" /> The rooms also contain tables made of cast concrete.<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 31" /> Many of these feature couches, [[kitchenettes]], and soaking tubs.<ref name="Vogue" /> The east-facing rooms look out over [[New Haven Harbor]], while the west-facing rooms look out over [[Downtown New Haven]].<ref name="Vogue" /><ref name="NPS p. 6" /> All except 14 of the guestrooms abut the facade<ref name="Webb f221" /> and have automated shades installed over their windows.<ref name="p3191365872" />

Closets and restrooms separated the office of Armstrong Rubber's president, along the southern facade, from the other rooms on that floor.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /> The former executive offices were converted into the hotel's largest guestrooms.<ref name="Vogue" /><ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 31" /> These units are decorated in a [[mid-century modern]] style,<ref name="St John t211" /> with gray and blue furnishings.<ref name="NPS p. 7" /> The wood paneling in the executive offices is retained in the modern eighth-story guestrooms.<ref name="Vogue" /><ref name="Arch 2" /><ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 31" />

== History ==
=== Development ===
The building was constructed as the headquarters of the Armstrong Rubber Company, a [[family business]] based in [[West Haven, Connecticut]].<ref name="NPS p. 12">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2021|ps=.|page=12}}</ref><ref name="Gatje pp. 209–210">{{harvnb|Gatje|2000|ps=.|pages=209–210}}</ref> The company became a major supplier of tires for the [[Sears and Roebuck]] chain<ref name="Gatje pp. 209–210" /><ref name="n1867045512">{{Cite news |last=Rasie |first=Lawrence B. |date=September 13, 1988 |title=After 1980s overhaul, Armtek more profitable, more attractive |via=Newspapers.com |url-access=limited|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/hartford-courant-after-1980s-overhaul-a/186704551/ |access-date=December 12, 2025 |work=Hartford Courant |pages=C1, [https://www.newspapers.com/article/hartford-courant-after-1980s-overhaul-a/186704619/ C3]}}</ref> and was among the world's largest tire manufacturers by the 1960s.<ref name="NPS p. 12" /> Under the tenure of its vice president Joseph R. Armstrong, the company sought to expand.<ref name="Gatje p. 210">{{harvnb|Gatje|2000|ps=.|page=210}}</ref><ref name="NPS p. 11">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2021|ps=.|page=11}}</ref>

==== Design ====
[[File:Pirelli Building NH.jpg|thumb|Southeast-facing view showing air gap and columns under the building's upper stories]]
The site of the Armstrong Rubber Company Building was originally reserved for [[Marlin Firearms]] as part of the development of New Haven's Long Wharf district. Marlin Firearms rejected the site because it was too small for the company's future development, instead deciding to build elsewhere.<ref name="n186632974">{{Cite news |date=June 11, 1966 |title=Armstrong Rubber Plans Headquarters |via=Newspapers.com |url-access=limited|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/connecticut-post-armstrong-rubber-plans/186632974/ |access-date=December 11, 2025 |work=Connecticut Post |pages=7 |agency=Associated Press}}</ref> Armstrong Rubber announced plans to move its corporate offices to Long Wharf in June 1966.<ref name="n186632974" /><ref name="p549161149">{{cite news |date=June 12, 1966 |title=$5 Million Move |work=The Hartford Courant |page=16A |id={{ProQuest|549161149}}}}</ref> That year, Armstrong Rubber presented plans to New Haven Mayor [[Richard C. Lee]] for a building overlooking the interchange between [[Interstate 91]] and [[Interstate 95 in Connecticut|Interstate 95]].<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /><ref name="hyperallergic.com">{{Cite web |last=Fritchley |first=Sarah |date=August 10, 2017 |title=Placing Pieces of Local History in an Empty Marcel Breuer Building |url=https://hyperallergic.com/placing-pieces-of-local-history-in-an-empty-marcel-breuer-building/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190209124604/https://hyperallergic.com/394885/placing-pieces-of-local-history-in-an-empty-marcel-breuer-building/ |archive-date=February 9, 2019 |access-date=February 9, 2019 |website=Hyperallergic |language=en-US}}</ref> Lee allowed the purchase on the condition that a world-renowned architect be hired.<ref name="Gatje p. 210" /><ref name="hyperallergic.com" /><ref name="McCarter p. 383">{{harvnb|McCarter|2024|ps=.|page=383}}</ref> Lee's preference of architect had come about because the site was highly visible from the nearby highway,<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /><ref name="Gatje p. 210" /> and Lee wanted New Haven to become "America's model city".<ref name="hyperallergic.com" />

Armstrong Rubber hired the Hungarian-American architect [[Marcel Breuer]] after a year-long search.<ref name="hyperallergic.com" /> Breuer was selected because his design for the [[Robert C. Weaver Federal Building]] had been completed on time and under budget,<ref name="NPS p. 12" /><ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> and the architect had also recently completed the [[UNESCO Headquarters]] and [[945 Madison Avenue]].<ref name="p1852421416" /> Initially, Armstrong wanted a low-rise building to house its [[research and development]] department and its offices.<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /><ref name="McCarter p. 383" /> The office section, near the highway, would have risen two or three stories, while the research and development wing behind it would have been shorter.<ref name="Gatje p. 211">{{harvnb|Gatje|2000|ps=.|page=211}}</ref> Lee rejected Armstrong's low-rise proposal because only the massive roof would be visible to drivers on the highway, given the site's depressed position.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /><ref name="NPS p. 12" /> Instead, he suggested that the building be between eight and ten stories high;<ref name="Docomomo" /><ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /> according to Breuer's associate [[Robert F. Gatje]], Lee had initially wanted an even taller building with 18 stories.<ref name="Gatje p. 211" />

Breuer drew up plans for a two-story [[research and development]] space and a five-story office section with a mechanical penthouse, separated by a double-height air gap.<ref name="Gatje pp. 211–212" /><ref name="NPS p. 12" /><ref name="Gatje p. 211" /> The upper stories would have been square.<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /> The air gap not only raised the office stories, allowing them to face [[New Haven Harbor]] directly, but also attracted notice because the feature was so uncommon.<ref name="NPS pp. 12–13">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2021|ps=.|pages=12–13}}</ref> Preliminary designs were completed in August 1967.<ref>{{Cite news |date=August 5, 1967 |title=New Haven's Newest |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/hartford-courant-new-havens-newest/186635138/ |url-access=limited |access-date=December 11, 2025 |work=Hartford Courant |pages=13 |via=Newspapers.com}}</ref> At Armstrong's insistence, Breuer removed one of the office stories from the plans.<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /><ref name="Gatje p. 213">{{harvnb|Gatje|2000|ps=.|page=213}}</ref><ref name="NPS p. 13">{{harvnb|National Park Service|2021|ps=.|page=13}}</ref> The final plans called for a two-story base with the offices suspended above it;<ref name="Docomomo" /><ref name="n186633165" /> by Breuer's estimate, the suspended upper section increased costs by five to ten percent.<ref name="Gatje p. 212">{{harvnb|Gatje|2000|ps=.|page=212}}</ref> The plans also called for a poured and precast concrete facade, which was to vary in appearance depending on the sun's position in the sky.<ref name="NPS p. 6" /><ref name="n186633165" />

==== Construction ====
The Dwight Building Company was hired in January 1968 to construct the building, beating out six other bidders.<ref name="n186633165">{{Cite news |date=January 27, 1968 |title=Armstrong Selects Building Contractor |via=Newspapers.com |url-access=limited|url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-hanford-sentinel-armstrong-selects-b/186633165/ |access-date=December 11, 2025 |work=The Hanford Sentinel |pages=15}}</ref> The building was largely constructed with a steel frame, except for a portion of the building that was constructed with long ceiling beams made of concrete.<ref name="McCarter p. 383" /><ref name="Gatje p. 212" /> The concrete facade was made of white cement, which was sandblasted to expose the dark [[Aggregate (composite)|aggregate]] within.<ref name="Gatje p. 212" />

During the building's construction, Armstrong Rubber's board of directors asked Breuer to design signage for the building's roof, but Breuer refused the request, saying it would detract from the building's shape.<ref name="Gatje p. 213"/> As an alternative, Breuer designed a freestanding sign elsewhere on the property,<ref name="McCarter p. 384" /><ref name="Gatje p. 213" /> and he added a door to the sign to circumvent height restrictions that applied to signs in the area.<ref name="Gatje pp. 213–214" /> The Armstrong Rubber Building was completed in May 1970;<ref name="p550476808">{{cite news |date=May 3, 1970 |title=Armstrong Rubber Co. Has New Headquarters |work=The Hartford Courant |page=7G |id={{ProQuest|550476808}}}}</ref> the building's final cost was variously cited at $6.5 million{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=6.5|start_year=1970|r=0|fmt=c}} million in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="1969 AF" /><ref name="p550191037" /> or $7 million.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=7|start_year=1970|r=0|fmt=c}} million in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="p550476808" /><ref name="n186701186">{{Cite news |date=March 29, 1971 |title=American Can Tops List of State Building Jobs |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/connecticut-post-american-can-tops-list/186701186/ |url-access=limited |access-date=December 12, 2025 |work=Connecticut Post |pages=8 |via=Newspapers.com |agency=Associated Press}}</ref> It was the second-most-expensive building completed in Connecticut that year, after the [[American Can Company]] headquarters building in [[Greenwich, Connecticut|Greenwich]], which had cost $30 million.{{efn-ur|Equivalent to ${{Inflation|index=US-GDP|value=30|start_year=1970|r=0|fmt=c}} million in {{Inflation/year|index=US-GDP}}{{Inflation/fn|index=US-GDP}}}}<ref name="n186701186" /> The building originally covered a {{Convert|19|acre|adj=on}} site.<ref name="Metropolis v381" /><ref name="n186656112" />

The building reopened as the 165-room Hotel Marcel on May 16, 2022;<ref name="Zaretsky 2022">{{Cite news |last=Zaretsky |first=Mark |date=May 16, 2022 |title=New Haven's Pirelli Building transformed to Hotel Marcel |url=https://www.nhregister.com/news/article/New-Haven-s-Pirelli-Building-transformed-to-17171927.php |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251011201647/https://www.nhregister.com/news/article/New-Haven-s-Pirelli-Building-transformed-to-17171927.php |archive-date=October 11, 2025 |access-date=December 12, 2025 |work=New Haven Register |language=en}}</ref> the name referenced the building's primary architect.<ref name="Sullivan h787" /> The upper-level spaces were not complete until several weeks after the hotel's opening.<ref name="Sullivan h787" /> The Hotel Marcel is managed by Charlestowne Hotels<ref name="St John t211" /> and is part of [[Hilton Worldwide|Hilton]]'s Tapestry brand.<ref name="AR 2022" /><ref name="Prevost r206" /> In addition to its green-building design features, the hotel relied exclusively on solar energy,<ref name="Stephens y349" /><ref name="Zeitoun e204" /> promoting itself as a "fossil fuel-free hotel".<ref name="NYT 2025 x789">{{cite web |last1=Hirschfeld |first1=Cindy |last2=Miller |first2=Andrew |date=April 22, 2025 |title=A New 'Carbon-Positive' Hotel in Denver Takes Sustainability a Step Further |url-access=limited |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2025/04/22/travel/denver-sustainable-hotels-populus.html |access-date=December 12, 2025 |website=The New York Times |archive-date=August 25, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250825233235/https://www.nytimes.com/2025/04/22/travel/denver-sustainable-hotels-populus.html |url-status=live }}</ref> The hotel also implemented several policies to reduce waste, including obtaining food supplies from local sources, eliminating table linens,<ref name="Fox e278" /><ref name="Moore 2024">{{Cite news |last=Moore |first=Elizabeth L. T. |title='Hell's Kitchen' finalist brings Southern roots to sustainable restaurant at Hotel Marcel New Haven |url=https://www.nhregister.com/news/article/megan-gill-bldg-hotel-marcel-new-haven-ct-19460098.php |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240901131155/https://www.nhregister.com/news/article/megan-gill-bldg-hotel-marcel-new-haven-ct-19460098.php |archive-date=September 1, 2024 |access-date=December 12, 2025 |work=New Haven Register |language=en}}</ref> [[composting]] leftover food, using only recyclable or reusable products, and washing towels only on demand.<ref name="Kuehner-Hebert x146" /><ref name="Beketova x312">{{cite web |last=Beketova |first=Zoe |date=September 19, 2024 |title=CT's ugliest building transformed into one of America's most sustainable |url=https://yaledailynews.com/blog/2024/09/19/cts-ugliest-building-transformed-into-one-of-americas-most-sustainable/ |access-date=December 12, 2025 |website=Yale Daily News |archive-date=May 25, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250525051008/https://yaledailynews.com/blog/2024/09/19/cts-ugliest-building-transformed-into-one-of-americas-most-sustainable/ |url-status=live }}</ref>

== Impact ==

=== Reception ===
When the Armstrong Rubber Company Building opened, it was called "an icon of modern architecture" in ''[[ArchitectureWeek]]'' magazine.<ref name="p749945507" /><ref name="n186651538" /> The historian [[Elizabeth Mills Brown]] wrote in a 1976 guidebook of New Haven buildings that the massing "was designed to create an instantly striking image of light and shadow when seen from a speeding car".<ref name="Brown q486" /> Robert Nieminen of ''Buildings'' magazine wrote that the air gap gave the impression that the upper stories were floating,<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /> and ''[[The Daily Telegraph|The Telegraph]]'' wrote that the building was "{{As written|recognis|able}} for the top-heavy look" created by the gap.<ref name="Telegraph k207" /> The ''Architectural Record'' wrote that the design had been "formally distorted for symbolic effect", being akin to what the architects [[Robert Venturi]] and [[Denise Scott Brown]] referred to as "ducks".<ref name="AR 2022" /> ''The Plan'' magazine described the building's shape as having served as a landmark from the outset,<ref name="Webb f221" /> and ''Wallpaper'' magazine said the shape epitomized "Breuer's approach to separation of function".<ref name="Scheffler w466" /> The Connecticut Historical Commission wrote that the gap gave the building the impression that it was "bridging the highway", thereby connecting New Haven and the waterfront.<ref name="CHC p. 3">{{harvnb|Connecticut Historical Commission|2000|ps=.|page=3}}</ref>[[File:IKEA-Pirelli-Building-New-Haven-Connecticut-04-2014.jpg|thumb|Under IKEA ownership in 2014]]The Connecticut chapter of the [[American Institute of Architects]] and the preservation group [[Docomomo]] viewed the building favorably when its redevelopment was proposed in the 2000s.<ref name="NYT" /> After the neighboring IKEA store was developed, ''[[The Financial Times]]'' and the ''[[Architectural Record]]'' wrote that the structure's placement next to IKEA was "incongruous",<ref name="AR 2022" /><ref name="Usborne a213">{{cite web |last=Usborne |first=Simon |date=May 31, 2019 |title=Brutal luxury: London's new Standard hotel |url=https://www.ft.com/content/d7f32b66-8087-11e9-a7f0-77d3101896ec |access-date=December 10, 2025 |website=Financial Times |url-access=subscription |archive-date=March 22, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230322230437/https://www.ft.com/content/d7f32b66-8087-11e9-a7f0-77d3101896ec |url-status=live }}</ref> and ''Dwell'' magazine said of IKEA's parking-lot plans: "Is it really so important to be able to park near the latest in mass-designed Swedish decor?"<ref name="Dwell 2003" /> The ''New Haven Register'' called it an "empty [[white elephant]] with little hope of 'logical reuse{{'"}} in 2009,<ref name="p457141888">{{Cite news |last=Amarante |first=Joe |date=November 12, 2009 |title=A look at CT buildings we know and, eh, like |work=McClatchy-Tribune Business News |id={{ProQuest|457141888}}}}</ref> and another writer for the same newspaper wrote in 2011 that the "once proud and even sexily zesty building responding to high-speed traffic has become a symbol of sad uselessness".<ref name="p879069652" /> ''[[The Hartford Courant]]'' wrote in 2016 that "this architectural statement was the full expression of New Haven's mid-century full-on embrace of urban renewal".<ref name="p1852421416" /> After the hotel plans were announced, [[Inga Saffron]] of the ''[[Philadelphia Inquirer]]'' said the plans signified a resurgence in popularity for brutalism.<ref name="Saffron2020">{{Cite web |last=Saffron |first=Inga |date=January 23, 2020 |title=The 'Clothespin' is liberated. Now can Centre Square's renovations make us love Brutalist design? |url=https://www.inquirer.com/real-estate/inga-saffron/centre-square-architecture-brutalism-renovation-atrium-kling-oldenburg-clothespin-dubuffet-inga-saffron-20200123.html |access-date=December 12, 2025 |website=Philadelphia Inquirer |language=en |archive-date=February 17, 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260217153118/https://www.inquirer.com/real-estate/inga-saffron/centre-square-architecture-brutalism-renovation-atrium-kling-oldenburg-clothespin-dubuffet-inga-saffron-20200123.html |url-status=live }}</ref>

When the building reopened as a hotel, the ''Record-Journal'' called it "an architectural symbol of its time",<ref name="n186646870" /> and the [[Michelin Guide]] wrote that the hotel's opening showed that "Brutalism is back in style", with a distinctive interior that honored the original design rather than copying it outright.<ref name="MICHELIN Guide">{{cite web |title=Hotel Marcel involvement in the automotive industry.<ref name="Tucker e785" /> ''The Financial Times'' placed the Hotel Marcel on its list of "Architecture to See in 2023", as a striking Brutalist landmark and a successful renovation following the then-recent renovation of [[945 Madison Avenue]] for the [[Frick Collection]].<ref>{{cite news |last=Heathcote |first=Edwin |title=Architecture to see in 2023 |work=Financial Times |url-access=subscription |url=https://www.ft.com/content/495b0390-2567-4846-8680-0904febdf395 |date=December 28, 2022 |access-date=February 19, 2023 |archive-date=February 19, |archive-date=November 12, 2025 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251112150447/https://www.wsj.com/lifestyle/travel/the-modernist-architecture-mecca-you-never-knew-existed-right-off-i-95-d3de5219 |url-status=live }}</ref> That year, ''[[Condé Nast Traveler]]'' labeled it as [[New England]]'s fourth-best hotel.<ref name="Rosenberg 2025">{{Cite news |last=Rosenberg |first=Nathaniel |title=Seven CT hotels among best in |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20251011201515/https://www.ctinsider.com/living/article/conde-nast-traveler-top-ct-hotels-new-england-21089346.php |archive-date=October 11, 2025 |access-date=December 12, 2025 |work=CT Insider |language=en}}</ref>

In contrast to its positive architectural reception, the Armstrong Rubber Company Building was more controversial among the public.<ref name="n186646870" /><ref name="NPS pp. 5–6" /> A ''[[Business Insider]]'' survey of Connecticut residents in 2018 had ranked the building as the state's ugliest.<ref name="Arch 2" /><ref>{{Cite web |last=Dayton |first=Kels |date=July 25, 2018 |title=Pirelli Building in New Haven named Connecticut's ugliest building |url=https://www.wtnh.com/news/connecticut/pirelli-building-in-new-haven-named-connecticut-s-ugliest-building/1321618221 |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190209131257/https://www.wtnh.com/news/connecticut/pirelli-building-in-new-haven-named-connecticut-s-ugliest-building/1321618221 |archive-date=February 9, 2019 |access-date=February 9, 2019 |website=Wtnh.com |language=en-US}}</ref>{{Efn|For the poll itself, see: {{cite web | last=Garfield | first=Leanna | title=The ugliest building in every US state, according to people who live there | website=Business Insider | date=October 12, 2018 | url=https://www.businessinsider.com/ugliest-buildings-in-the-us-2018-1 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190209124026/https://www.businessinsider.com/ugliest-buildings-in-the-us-2018-1 | archive-date=February 9, 2019 | url-status=live | access-date=December 11, 2025}}}} After the hotel was completed, Yale architecture professor Violette De La Selle said, "Out there in the world, this is either the best or the worst building in Connecticut."<ref name="Sullivan h787" /> The Connecticut Historical Commission's report on the building stated that the building had been completed just as the public was turning away from modernist buildings, and that the Armstrong Rubber Building had been overshadowed by 945 Madison Avenue as Breuer's best major building.<ref name="CHC p. 13">{{harvnb|Connecticut Historical Commission|2000|ps=.|page=13}}</ref>

=== Awards and landmark designations ===
When plans for a mall on the site began to take traction, the possibility of the demolition of the Pirelli Tire Building led to efforts to list the site on the Connecticut Register of Historic Places.<ref name="Mall" /> It was listed on the state register in 1997,<ref name="Charles e752" /><ref>{{Cite news |title=BEACH: New Haven's loneliest building needs visionary |url=http://www.nhregister.com:80/news/article/BEACH-New-Haven-s-loneliest-building-needs-11604244.php |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180118105720/http://www.nhregister.com:80/news/article/BEACH-New-Haven-s-loneliest-building-needs-11604244.php |archive-date=January 18, 2018 |access-date=December 14, 2025 |work=New Haven Register}}</ref>{{Efn|The state register listing is cited in one source as having occurred in 2000.<ref name="Keh 2021" />}} following efforts by city officials, preservationists, and the New Haven Arts Council's Alliance for Architecture.<ref name="hyperallergic.com" /><ref name="Revival" /> The state register listing did not legally prevent the structure from being demolished.<ref name="Charles e752" /> The Hotel Marcel building was additionally listed on the [[National Register of Historic Places]] in 2021.<ref name="weekly">{{Cite web |year=2021 |title=National Register of Historic Places Weekly Lists 2021 |url=https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nationalregister/upload/Weekly-List-2021_revised20240523-508.pdf |access-date=December 12, 2025 |publisher=National Park Service |page=76 |archive-date=March 1, 2026 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20260301041810/https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nationalregister/upload/Weekly-List-2021_revised20240523-508.pdf |url-status=live }}</ref> As a result of these designations, the hotel renovation required review from both state and federal preservation officials,<ref name="Stephens y349" /> and the renovation was eligible for [[tax credit]]s.<ref name="Webb f221" />

After the hotel conversion was completed, the building received a [[LEED]] Platinum [[green building]] certification (the highest designation granted by LEED),<ref name="Stephens y349" /><ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /> making it one of the few LEED Platinum–certified hotels in the US.<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /><ref name="Docomomo-nytri.org" /> A plaque commemorating this certification is mounted outside the building.<ref name="Bass u228" /> The Hotel Marcel was the US's first hotel certified as having [[net-zero emissions]].<ref name="Nieminen 2022 p. 30" /><ref name="p2477254921" /> The hotel received a [[Passive House]] green building certification from the Passive House Institute,<ref name="Stephens y349" /> making it one of 140 buildings in the nation with that certification, as well as the world's first Passive House hotel with more than 100 rooms.<ref name="p3191365872" /> Separately, the hotel won ''[[Boutique Design]]'' magazine's 2022 Gold Key award for eco- or socially conscious hotels.<ref name="auto"/> The adaptive-reuse project also received a 2022 Award of Excellence from Docomomo US and a 2023 Award of Merit from Preservation Connecticut.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Racioppi |first=Michele |date=September 12, 2022 |title=Announcing the winners of the 2022 Modernism in America Awards |url=https://docomomo-us.org/news/announcing-the-winners-of-the-2022-modernism-in-america-awards |access-date=September 1, 2026 |website=Docomomo US}}</ref><ref>{{Cite press release |date=April 12, 2023 |title=For immediate release: Connecticut Preservation Awards 2023 |url=https://preservationct.org/for-immediate-release-connecticut-preservation-awards-2023 |access-date=September 1, 2026 |website=Preservation Connecticut}}</ref>
