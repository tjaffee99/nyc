'''Colross''' (also historically known as '''Belle Air''' and '''Grasshopper Hall''') is a [[Georgian architecture|Georgian]] style [[Plantation house in the Southern United States|mansion]] built around 1800 as the center of a [[Plantation complexes in the Southern United States|large plantation]] in what is now the [[Old Town Alexandria|Old Town]] neighborhood of [[Alexandria, Virginia]], United States, and moved circa 1930 to [[Princeton, New Jersey]], where it is currently the administration building of [[Princeton Day School]].

The Colross property originally occupied the entire 1100 block of Oronoco Street; Alexandria merchant John Potts developed it as a plantation and began building the mansion in 1799{{ndash}}1800. In 1803, Jonathan Swift—also an Alexandria merchant and a city councilman—purchased the property and during his ownership continued constructing the mansion. After Swift died in 1824, Colross was purchased by [[Thomson Francis Mason]], son of [[Thomson Mason (1759–1820)|Thomson Mason]] and grandson of Founding Father [[George Mason]] of [[Gunston Hall]]. Mason served as a judge of the Criminal Court of the District of Columbia and as mayor of Alexandria. Mason, who made Colross his chief homestead, modified and enlarged the mansion. Colross served as the venue for several significant [[Mason family]] events,. According to local tradition, two children in the Mason family died on the property and were interred in the estate's burial vault. Successive owners of the Colross estate claimed it was haunted by the deceased Mason children. After successive ownerships, the area around Colross became heavily industrialized. The mansion was bought by John Munn in 1929; between that year and 1932, it was transported brick-by-brick to Princeton, where in 1958 it was sold to Princeton Day School, which uses it as a school administration building housing its admission and advancement offices.

The Colross mansion is a two-story, brick, Georgian-style structure that features an architectural plan similar to that of [[Mount Vernon]] and [[Woodlawn (Alexandria, Virginia)|Woodlawn]], and it was originally flanked by two wings. The front entrance is covered by a spacious [[Neoclassical architecture|Neoclassical]] [[portico]] that is supported by wooden [[Doric column]]s. The roof is topped by a [[Baluster|balustraded]] deck and is further embellished by three [[dormer]] windows.

In 2005, after the original Colross site was purchased by a real estate development company, the city of Alexandria requested an excavation by archaeologists, who uncovered an underground domed brick [[cistern]], evidence of slave outbuildings, the foundations of the estate's peripheral walls, and several ancillary structures.

==History==

===Virginia===
The land on which Colross was first located was originally part of the [[Northern Neck Proprietary]], a land grant that the exiled [[Charles II of England|Charles II]] awarded to seven of his supporters in 1649 during the [[Interregnum (England)|English Interregnum]].<ref name="Munske1">{{Harvnb|Munske|Kerns|2004|p=9.}}</ref><ref name="Coleman1">{{Harvnb|Coleman|1951|p=246.}}</ref><ref name="Rose 1">{{Harvnb|Rose|1976|p=25.}}</ref> Following the [[Restoration (England)|Restoration]] in 1660, Charles II finally ascended to the English throne.<ref name="W&MQ1">{{Harvnb|''William and Mary Quarterly''|1898|p=222.}}</ref> Charles II renewed the Northern Neck Proprietary grant in 1662, revised it in 1669, and again renewed the original grant favoring original grantee [[Thomas Colepeper, 2nd Baron Colepeper]] and [[Henry Bennet, 1st Earl of Arlington]] in 1672.<ref name="W&MQ2">{{Harvnb|''William and Mary Quarterly''|1898|pp=222–3.}}</ref> In 1681, Bennet sold his share to Lord Colepeper, and Lord Colepeper received a new charter for the entire land grant from [[James II of England|James II]] in 1688.<ref name="Munske1" /><ref name="W&MQ3">{{Harvnb|''William and Mary Quarterly''|1898|p=224.}}</ref> Following the deaths of Lord Colepeper, his wife Margaret, and his daughter Katherine, the Northern Neck Proprietary passed to Katherine's son [[Thomas Fairfax, 6th Lord Fairfax of Cameron]] in 1719.<ref name="Munske1" /><ref name="W&MQ4">{{Harvnb|''William and Mary Quarterly''|1898|pp=224–6.}}</ref><ref name="Rice1">{{Harvnb|Rice|2015|p=23.}}</ref>

continued to construct the mansion.<ref name="RMC1"/> After his death in 1824, the estate transferred to the ownership of Lee Massey Alexander and his sister, Mrs. Chapman. The Alexander family owned the estate for a brief period; they renamed it "Colross".<ref name = "Lee1"/>

Colross was then purchased by [[Thomson Francis Mason]], a prominent [[jurist]], lawyer, councilman, judge of the Criminal Court of the District of Columbia, and mayor of Alexandria between 1827 and 1830.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> Mason was the eldest son of [[Thomson Mason (1759–1820)|Thomson Mason]], and was the grandson of U.S. Founding Father [[George Mason]] of [[Gunston Hall]].<ref name = "Lee1"/> According to Mason's daughter Virginia Mason Davidge, her father won Colross "at a game of cards" from Lee Massey Alexander.<ref name = "Lee1"/><ref "Brown1">{{Harvnb|Brown|2013|p=10.}}</ref> Mason built a {{convert|10|ft|abbr=on|adj=on}} high brick wall around the exterior of the Colross property.<ref name = "Lee1"/><ref name = "Taylor3"/> Around the same time Mason acquired Colross, he built [[Huntley (plantation)|Huntley]] in [[Fairfax County, Virginia|Fairfax County]], Virginia as a rural retreat and summer villa.<ref name=HH/><ref name=NRHP/><ref name = "Brown1"/> Mason's son, [[Arthur Pendleton Mason|Arthur "Pen" Pendleton Mason]], later inherited the Colross estate. Pen Mason was married to Mary Ellen Campbell, a daughter of [[John Archibald Campbell]], an [[Associate Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States]].<ref name = "Harrison1">{{Harvnb|Harrison|1912|p=154.}}</ref> [[Orlando B. Willcox]], who later served as a [[Union Army]] general, visited Colross on several occasions around 1851; he described it as a "fine house and ground and the chief residence of the Masons of Alexandria, much frequented by officers of the army". Willcox also remarked on the "hospitality and civility of the head of the house", Pen Mason's mother, Elizabeth "Betsey" Clapham Price.<ref name = "Willcox1">{{Harvnb|Willcox|1999|p=169.}}</ref>

During the [[American Civil War]], Colross was seized by [[Union (American Civil War)|Union]] authorities.<ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name = "Combs1"/> According to local tradition and to Alexandria resident Julian Taylor, at least two Union deserters were executed with their backs against the estate's high brick exterior wall.<ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name = "Lee1"/><ref name = "Taylor3"/> In addition, a "famous '[[bounty jumper]]'" by the name of Downey was also shot and killed against the wall after being captured by his own soldiers.<ref name = "Lee1"/><ref name = "Taylor3"/> Local traditions also tell of the ghost of a soldier who haunts the former location of the estate's brick perimeter wall.<ref name = "Taylor3"/>

William Albert Smoot, a lumber merchant and coal businessman, purchased Colross from the Mason family and lived there with his family between 1885 and 1917.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name = "Combs1"/><ref name = "Lee2">{{Harvnb|Lee|2009|p=4.}}</ref> Smoot's wife was a member of the Alexander family, and was therefore a descendant of the estate's former owners.<ref name = "Taylor3"/><ref name = "Lee2"/><ref name = "Chapman1">{{Harvnb|Chapman|Calvert|Conlyn|1946|p=70.}}</ref> While there, the Smoots' daughter Betty wrote, "the grounds included a whole square block and were enclosed with an ancient brick wall ten feet in height".<ref name = "Taylor3"/> The Smoots' son William Albert later served as mayor of Alexandria from 1922 to 1930.<ref name = "Lee2"/>

The parents of [[Cornell University]] professor and activist [[Alice Cook (professor)|Alice Cook]] lived at the then-dilapidated Colross with her father's superior from the [[Southern Railway (U.S.)|Southern Railway]]. Her father worked for the railroad's bookkeeping department. Cook spent her early childhood at the house. In c. 1913, when Cook was about ten, her mother took her to Colross for a visit. She remarked that Colross "had no gaslights, and running water only in the kitchen", and that the house "stood in the midst of railroad tracks". Cook also said the house's adjacent stables still had horses, the "elegant plaster ceilings" remained intact, and "great oak doors" still stood within the house's main doorway behind the white columns of the front portico.<ref name = "Cook1">{{Harvnb|Cook|2000|p=1.}}</ref>

In 1917, another lumber merchant, William Hoge, acquired the mansion. Under similar circumstances to those of nearby [[Abingdon (plantation)|Abingdon]], properties surrounding Colross underwent industrialization with the construction of a warehouse complex and ancillary industrial buildings associated with Alexandria Hay & Grain.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> The mansion at Colross became a storage facility within a lumber yard operated by another [[planing mill]] owner.<ref name=DHR1/> In 1927, the mansion and the adjacent warehouses were substantially damaged by a tornado, making the mansion uninhabitable.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name="Metro Herald 1"/><ref name="Combs1"/><ref name="Chapman1"/>

===New Jersey===
Between 1929 and 1932, John Munn purchased the mansion, dismantled it, and shipped the structure brick-by-brick to [[Princeton, New Jersey]], where it was restored.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name=DHR1/><ref name = "Combs1"/> Following Munn's death in 1956, Colross was purchased by Dr. Geoffrey W. Rake. After Rake died in 1958, Colross was sold to [[Princeton Day School]].<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name=DHR1/> The mansion became the school's administrative building, which it remains to this day.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name=DHR1/> {{as of|2026}}, Colross houses the admission and advancement offices of Princeton Day School, and serves as a venue for the institution's events.<ref name=PDS1>{{cite web | url=http://www.pds.org/AtoZ?#colross | title=Only at Princeton Day School: PDS A to Z | year=2015 | access-date=October 6, 2015 | publisher=[[Princeton Day School]] | author=Princeton Day School | author-link=Princeton Day School | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20150907232509/http://www.pds.org/AtoZ | archive-date=September 7, 2015}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |title=How to Find Us - Princeton Day School |url=https://www.pds.org/admission/how-to-find-us |website=pds.org |access-date=5 July 2026}}</ref>

In Alexandria, the mansion's remaining brick foundation was buried beneath a slab of reinforced concrete for over 50 years.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/> Structures on the site have since included a large 50-truck garage, Andy's Car Wash, a [[Dominion Virginia Power]] [[Electrical substation|substation]], and the Hennage Creative Printers facility.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/>

== Architecture ==
[[File:Colross Alexandria VA 1916 04.jpg|thumb|right|[[Architectural drawing]] of the front elevation of Colross]]
The mansion at Colross was built in the [[Georgian architecture|Georgian]] architectural style<ref name=PDS1/> between 1799 and 1800. It is a two-story brick house featuring a rectangular architectural plan, which was originally flanked by two wings.<ref name = "RMC1"/><ref name = "Taylor3"/><ref name = "Lee3">{{Harvnb|Lee|2009|p=3 (following page with image of Colross).}}</ref> Colross features wide halls and spacious rooms.<ref name = "Taylor3"/><ref name = "Lee2"/> One wing housed facilities for the estate's service staff; the other wing served as a carriage shed.<ref name = "RMC1"/> Colross' architectural plan is similar to that of nearby estates [[Mount Vernon]] and [[Woodlawn (Alexandria, Virginia)|Woodlawn]], and is an example of the country house style of [[American colonial architecture]] common in Maryland and Virginia.<ref name = "RMC1"/> It has been described as the "largest and most beautiful mansion ever erected in Alexandria".<ref name = "Taylor1">{{Harvnb|Taylor|2010|p=28.}}</ref> The Colross property originally occupied the entire 1100 block of Oronoco Street.<ref name = "Taylor2">{{Harvnb|Taylor|2010|pp=28–29.}}</ref> The grounds of the estate also contained ancillary outbuildings.<ref name = "Taylor3"/>

The exterior brick walls of the mansion are laid in a [[Flemish bond]] pattern, exhibiting a "well proportioned width" of [[mortar joint]] between the bricks.<ref name = "RMC1"/> Two sets of double inside chimneys extend above the roofline on each side of the mansion's main structure.<ref name = "RMC1"/><ref name = "Lee3"/> The front façade of the mansion's main structure, which originally faced Oronoco Street in Alexandria, is five bays wide and contains the house's front entrance at the first floor's center bay.<ref name = "RMC1"/><ref name = "Lee3"/> The front entrance is covered by a spacious [[Neoclassical architecture]] style portico, which is supported by two sets of double wooden doric columns at the front and [[engaged column]]s on the brick façade.<ref name = "RMC1"/><ref name = "Lee3"/> The portico's [[frieze]] is subordinated to the [[architrave]].<ref name = "RMC1"/> The mansion's front door is topped by a leaded [[fanlight]] in the shape of a segmented arch.<ref name = "RMC1"/><ref name = "Lee3"/> Leaded [[sidelight]]s flank both sides of the main doorway.<ref name = "RMC1"/><ref name = "Lee3"/> All of the mansion's windows feature colonial-style [[Lintel (architecture)|lintels]].<ref name = "RMC1"/>

The mansion's roof is covered by gray slate and is further embellished with three [[dormer]] windows facing from the home's front façade.<ref name = "RMC1"/><ref name = "Lee3"/> The roof is topped by a [[Baluster|balustraded]] deck.<ref name = "RMC1"/> Similar moulding contours were used at varying scales throughout the mansion's exterior construction.<ref name = "RMC1"/> The mansion's [[cornice]] is composed of ornamented moldings.<ref name = "RMC1"/>

To the north of the mansion was a garden, which was purportedly well known for its [[Buxus|boxwoods]], [[Syringa vulgaris|lilacs]], and [[rose]]s.<ref name = "Lee2"/> The garden remained through the ownership of the Smoot family.<ref name = "Taylor3"/> A winding path led from the mansion to a large burial vault, which was closed by a great iron lock.<ref name = "Lee2"/> According to members of the Smoot family, the lock to the burial vault would "never stay locked more than three days" at a time.<ref name = "Taylor3"/><ref name = "Lee2"/> A vase of urn stood in the front lawn of the mansion; according to tradition this marked the location where [[Pocahontas]] was baptized.<ref name = "Chapman1"/> The urn remained in its location throughout the American Civil War and was later acquired by the [[Preservation Virginia|Association for the Preservation of Virginia Antiquities]].<ref name = "Chapman1"/>

==Archeological excavation==
The former Colross land tract on the 1100 block of Oronoco Street, which is bounded by North Fayette, Oronoco, Henry, and Pendleton streets,<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name=DHR1/><ref name = "Taylor2"/> was purchased in 2003 by Diamond Properties, a real estate development company with plans to build a mixed-use mid-rise luxury condominium project called Monarch Condominium.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name=WBJ>{{cite web | url=http://washington.bizjournals.com/washington/stories/2008/12/15/story16.html | title=Diamond Properties hit with multiple liens | date=December 16, 2008 | access-date=October 6, 2015 | work=[[American City Business Journals|Washington Business Journal]] | author=Melissa Castro | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20151006005219/http://www.bizjournals.com/washington/stories/2008/12/15/story16.html |archive-date = October 6, 2015}}</ref> In 2005, Alexandria's Archaeological Protection Code requirement forced Diamond Properties to halt its construction to allow for an archaeological excavation of the Colross site.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/><ref name=WBJ/> The excavation occurred between March and June 2005, as mandated by the city of Alexandria.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> Diamond Properties paid R. Christopher Goodwin & Associates Inc., a cultural resource management firm, about $100,000 to explore the site for historical artifacts and to ensure all burial plots had been removed.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/>

While only a few artifacts were recovered, historians said the dig offered a clearer view of early 19th-century life at Colross.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/> Discoveries included an underground domed brick [[cistern]] that served as a water purification system and evidence that enslaved people lived in outbuildings on the Colross estate.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> Archaeologists also discovered the mansion's original basement floor, which was laid in a [[Brickwork#Herringbone bond|herringbone bond]].<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> Evidence of the estate's exterior walls, the foundations of the [[smokehouse]], stables, and a burial vault were also unearthed.<ref name = "Washington Post 1"/><ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> In the northwestern portion of the property, what is thought to have been the foundation of a rectangular burial vault was found.<ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> No burial remains were discovered.<ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> All interments were presumably removed in the early 20th century.<ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> Thomson Francis Mason was originally interred at the Colross graveyard in 1838, as were two of his daughters. Subsequent residents had their remains reinterred at [[Christ Church (Alexandria, Virginia)|Christ Church]] Episcopal Cemetery in Alexandria.<ref name = "Metro Herald 1"/> According to the Alexandria Economic Development Partnership, the delay in construction caused 79 condominium buyers to abandon their purchases.<ref name=WBJ/>

==Significant Mason family events==
The Colross estate was the location for several significant events involving the Mason family. Sarah Elizabeth Mason, a daughter of Thomson Francis Mason and his wife Elizabeth Clapham Price, married St. George Tucker Campbell at Colross on November 17, 1841.<ref name=SEM>{{cite web | url=http://www.gunstonhall.org/masonweb/p4.htm#i162 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080611141315/http://www.gunstonhall.org/masonweb/p4.htm#i162 | archive-date=June 11, 2008 | title=Sarah Elizabeth Mason | year=2009 | access-date=March 9, 2009 | publisher=Gunston Hall website | author=Gunston Hall | author-link=Gunston Hall }}</ref> Virginia Mason, another daughter of Thomson and Elizabeth married William Hathorn Stewart Davidge at Colross on February 1, 1853.<ref name=VM>{{cite web | url=http://www.gunstonhall.org/masonweb/p4.htm#i167 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080611141315/http://www.gunstonhall.org/masonweb/p4.htm#i162 | archive-date=June 11, 2008 | title=Virginia Mason | year=2009 | access-date=March 9, 2009 | publisher=Gunston Hall website | author=Gunston Hall | author-link=Gunston Hall }}</ref> Colross was also the venue for the funeral of Mrs. Virginia King, wife of Dr. Benjamin King, on December 31, 1850.<ref name=VK>{{cite web | url=http://theoldentimes.com/virginiaking50pa_1.html | title=Virginia KING Death | year=2004 | access-date=October 6, 2015 | publisher=TheOldenTimes.com | author=TheOldenTimes.com | archive-url = https://web.archive.org/web/20151006005921/http://theoldentimes.com/virginiaking50pa_1.html | archive-date = October 6, 2015}}</ref> Mrs. King was a sister of Mrs. Judge Mason.<ref name=VK/>

According to local tradition, two small Mason children, William and Ann, were playing in the estate's yard when a storm arrived.<ref name="Taylor3"/><ref name="Lee2"/> William took shelter in the estate's chicken coop but the wind toppled the structure, killing him.<ref name="Taylor3"/><ref name="Lee2"/> Soon after William's death, his sister Ann drowned in a bathtub at Colross.<ref name="Taylor3"/><ref name="Lee2"/> Both children were interred in the estate's burial vault in the garden.<ref name="Taylor3"/><ref name="Lee2"/> Successive residents at Colross, including members of the Smoot family, claimed the estate was haunted by the deceased Mason children; they reported hearing children "giggling, singing, and talking", and witnessed apparitions of children in pre-Civil War attire.<ref name = "Taylor3"/>
