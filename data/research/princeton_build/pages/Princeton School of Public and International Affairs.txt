The '''Princeton School of Public and International Affairs''' (''abbrev.'' '''SPIA'''; formerly the '''Woodrow Wilson School of Public and International Affairs''') is a professional [[public policy school]] at [[Princeton University]]. The school provides an array of comprehensive coursework in the fields of [[international development]], [[foreign policy]], [[science]] and [[technology]], and [[economics]] and [[finance]] through its [[Undergraduate degree|undergraduate (AB) degrees]], graduate [[Master of Public Affairs]] (MPA), [[Master of Public Policy]] (MPP), and PhD degrees.

==History==
In 1930, Princeton University established the School of Public and International Affairs, which was originally meant to serve as an interdisciplinary program for undergraduate students in Princeton's liberal arts college. On February 23, 1930, the front page of ''The Sunday New York Times'' announced:<blockquote>Princeton Founds Statesmen's School – Institution Will Train Youths for Public Life and Will Stress Internationalism – Hoover Hails The Project. De W.C. Poole Quits Diplomatic Service to Be Its Liaison Officer With World Affairs. The establishment of a school of public and international affairs at Princeton University was announced today by President John Grier Hibben in his annual message to the National Alumni Association. The school's primary purpose is to train young men for public life and to equip them—and others—with a broad sense of "the fundamentals of citizenship."<ref name="InNatServ" /></blockquote>

President Hibben made the announcement in Nassau Hall, "where in 1783 George Washington received the thanks of the Continental Congress for his conduct of the Revolutionary War." [[DeWitt Clinton Poole]], a noted foreign service officer and "until recently counselor of the United States Embassy in Berlin" had been the prime advocate for the creation of the school.<ref name="InNatServ" /> The next year, in August 1931, the [[FIDAC|Fédération Interalliée Des Anciens Combattants]] (FIDAC), the most influential international veterans association of the interwar period (representing nine million [[Allies of World War I|Allied]] veterans), chose Princeton to receive its educational medal for promoting world peace and understanding.<ref name="InNatServ" />

In 1948, the school added a graduate professional program and was renamed to honor [[Woodrow Wilson]],<ref>{{cite web|title=Princeton Timeline|url=https://www.princeton.edu/main/about/history/timeline/}}</ref> who was the 13th president of the university, governor of [[New Jersey]] and the 28th president of the United States. In two of Wilson's speeches at the university – first during its 150th anniversary celebration in 1896 and again at his inauguration as the university's president in 1902 – he mentioned "Princeton in the nation's service."<ref>{{cite web|title=Princeton in the Nation's Service|url=http://etcweb.princeton.edu/CampusWWW/Companion/princeton_in_nations_service.html|access-date=2014-07-03|archive-date=2015-02-22|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150222010143/http://etcweb.princeton.edu/CampusWWW/Companion/princeton_in_nations_service.html|url-status=dead}}</ref> This phrase serves as the basis for the university's unofficial motto, which was amended in 2016 to "Princeton in the nation's service and in the service of humanity."<ref>{{cite web|title=Our History|url=http://wws.princeton.edu/about-wws/our-history}}</ref>

In 2015, student protesters forced Princeton to reconsider having the school named after Wilson due to his [[Woodrow Wilson and race|racist views]], of which they disapproved. The protesting efforts were largely due to the organizational efforts of the [[Black Justice League]]. Despite the group no longer existing, faculty and new student activists pled for the university to apologize to the original members to recognize the BJL's perseverance.<ref>{{Cite web |title=PART I {{!}} 'Resurfacing History': A Look Back at the Black Justice League's Campus Activism |url=https://aas.princeton.edu/news/part-i-resurfacing-history-look-back-black-justice-leagues-campus-activism |access-date=2022-12-13 |website=Department of African American Studies |language=en}}</ref> The Wilson Legacy Review Committee ultimately decided to keep his name attached to the school, observing that, as other notable men and women, Wilson had a "complex legacy of both positive and negative repercussions".<ref>{{cite web

But on June 26, 2020, following the eruption of [[George Floyd protests]] and the resurgence of the [[Black Lives Matter#2020|Black Lives Matter]] movement, the Princeton University board of trustees decided to rename the Woodrow Wilson School the "Princeton School of Public and International Affairs," citing [[Woodrow Wilson|Wilson]]'s "racist thinking and policies [that] make him an inappropriate namesake for a school or college whose scholars, students, and alumni must stand firmly against racism in all its forms."<ref>{{Cite web|title=U. renames Woodrow Wilson School and Wilson College|url=https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2020/06/woodrow-wilson-racism-school-and-college-renamed-black-justice-league|access-date=2020-06-27|website=The Princetonian}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|title=President Eisgruber's message to community on removal of Woodrow Wilson name from public policy school and Wilson College|url=https://www.princeton.edu/news/2020/06/27/president-eisgrubers-message-community-removal-woodrow-wilson-name-public-policy|access-date=2020-06-27|website=Princeton University|language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|title=Board of Trustees' decision on removing Woodrow Wilson's name from public policy school and residential college|url=https://www.princeton.edu/news/2020/06/27/board-trustees-decision-removing-woodrow-wilsons-name-public-policy-school-and|access-date=2020-06-27|website=Princeton University|language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite news|last=Pietsch|first=Bryan|date=2020-06-27|title=Princeton Will Remove Woodrow Wilson's Name From School|language=en-US|work=The New York Times|url=https://www.nytimes.com/2020/06/27/nyregion/princeton-university-woodrow-wilson.html|access-date=2020-06-27|issn=0362-4331}}</ref> It was also announced that Wilson College, the first of Princeton's six undergraduate residential colleges, would be renamed [[First College]]. Woodrow Wilson opposed admitting African-American students to Princeton, and introduced racial segregation into the [[United States federal civil service]] as president.<ref>{{Cite news|last=Slotkin|first=Jason|date=2020-06-27|title=Princeton to Remove Woodrow Wilson's Name From Public Policy School|language=en|publisher=NPR|url=https://www.npr.org/sections/live-updates-protests-for-racial-justice/2020/06/27/884310403/princeton-to-remove-woodrow-wilsons-name-from-public-policy-school|access-date=2022-03-30}}</ref>

From 2012 to 2021, [[Cecilia Rouse]] served as dean of the Princeton School until her confirmation as Chair of the [[Council of Economic Advisors]] under the Biden Administration.

==Academics==

===Undergraduate program===
The school stresses a multidisciplinary approach to policy studies with undergraduate students at the Princeton School taking courses in at least four disciplines including economics, history, politics, psychology, sociology, and science policy.<ref>{{cite web|title=Undergraduate Academics|url=http://wws.princeton.edu/undergraduate-academics|access-date=2014-07-03|archive-date=2014-07-03|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140703121636/http://wws.princeton.edu/undergraduate-academics|url-status=dead}}</ref> In their junior year, students must enroll in and complete a Policy Task Force, which addresses a specific public policy issue. Students conduct research, propose recommendations, and issue final reports.<ref>{{cite web|title=Policy Task Forces|url=http://wws.princeton.edu/undergraduate-academics/curriculum/policy-task-forces|access-date=2014-07-03|archive-date=2014-07-14|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140714161112/http://wws.princeton.edu/undergraduate-academics/curriculum/policy-task-forces|url-status=dead}}</ref> Students are also allowed to work towards certificates in an array of fields, including [[Health policy|Global Health and Health Policy]], [[Urban Studies]], and the [[Princeton University Department of History|History and the Practice of Diplomacy]].

===Master's degrees===
The two-year MPA program prepares students for international and domestic policy careers. All second-year MPA students must complete a Policy Workshop, which emphasizes policy implementation. Through the graduate policy workshops, students conduct field-based research and present their research and recommendations to clients. Students also develop analytical and quantitative skills through coursework emphasizing the political, economic, and behavioral aspects of complex policy issues. MPA candidates may select one of four fields of concentration: 
* [[International Relations]]
* [[International Development]]
* [[Domestic policy|Domestic Policy]]
* [[Economic policy|Economics and Public Policy]]

The one-year MPP program is designed for mid-career professionals, PhD research scientists, lawyers, and physicians who are involved in international and domestic public policy.

===Doctoral degrees===
The PhD in public affairs focuses on two research areas: security studies; and science, technology and environmental policy. The school works with other departments at the university to offer a joint degree program that combines work in a social science with a multidisciplinary perspective on economics problems.<ref>{{cite web|title=Graduate Academics|url=http://wws.princeton.edu/graduate-academics|access-date=2014-07-03|archive-date=2014-07-03|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140703172640/http://wws.princeton.edu/graduate-academics|url-status=dead}}</ref> Graduate students also have the opportunity to pursue certificates in demography; health and health policy; science, technology and environmental policy; and urban policy/urban policy and planning.<ref>{{cite web|title=Programs|url=http://wws.princeton.edu/graduate-academics/programs-and-certificates|access-date=2014-07-03|archive-date=2014-07-03|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140703112447/http://wws.princeton.edu/graduate-academics/programs-and-certificates|url-status=dead}}</ref> In addition to the MPA, MPP and PhD degrees,<ref>{{cite web|title=Joint MPA/JD|url=http://wws.princeton.edu/graduate-academics/programs/joint-mpajd|access-date=2014-07-03|archive-date=2014-07-15|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140715122619/http://wws.princeton.edu/graduate-academics/programs/joint-mpajd|url-status=dead}}</ref> the school offers a four-year MPA/J.D. program, and has formal joint degree arrangements with law schools at [[Columbia University]],<ref>{{cite web|title=Joint Degree Programs|url=http://web.law.columbia.edu/admissions/jd/learn/programming|publisher=Columbia Law School|access-date=2014-07-03|archive-date=2014-07-14|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140714213123/http://web.law.columbia.edu/admissions/jd/learn/programming|url-status=dead}}</ref> [[New York University]]<ref>{{cite web|title=Dual Degree with Woodrow Wilson School of Public and International Affairs at Princeton University|url=https://www.law.nyu.edu/jdadmissions/dualdegreeprograms/princetonuniversity|publisher=NYU Law}}</ref> and [[Stanford University]].<ref>{{cite web|title=Overview of Joint Degree and Cooperative Programs|url=https://www.law.stanford.edu/degrees/joint/joint-degrees-within-stanford-university|publisher=Stanford Law School}}</ref> Students often refer to the Princeton School by its colloquial abbreviation, "SPIA". The school also offers a joint degree program (JDP) in social policy, allowing students to take courses in the departments of politics, psychology, sociology, and economics.

==Robertson Hall==
[[File:Woodrow Wilson School of Public and International Affairs - Robertson Hall.jpg|thumb|230px|right|Robertson Hall with [[James FitzGerald (artist)|James FitzGerald]]'s ''Fountain of Freedom'' in the foreground]]

In 1961, Charles '26 and Marie Robertson provided a gift to expand the graduate school. Their gift funded the construction of the School's current home, designed by [[Minoru Yamasaki]], who also designed New York's original [[World Trade Center (1973–2001)|World Trade Center]].<ref>{{cite web|last1=Zielenziger|first1=David|title=Miss The World Trade Center? Princeton's Robertson Hall Remains Reminder|url=https://www.ibtimes.com/miss-world-trade-center-princetons-robertson-hall-remains-reminder-780799|website=International Business Times|date=10 September 2012}}</ref> To the north of the building is [[James FitzGerald (artist)|James FitzGerald]]'s ''Fountain of Freedom'' (1966), colloquially the "SPIA fountain".<ref>{{cite web|title=Fountain of Freedom|url=https://artmuseum.princeton.edu/campus-art/objects/45571|website=Campus Art at Princeton|publisher=Princeton Art Museum|access-date=11 August 2016}}</ref> "Before construction could begin [on Robertson], the building that had housed the School since 1952, then called Woodrow Wilson School Annex, had to be relocated."<ref name="InNatServ">{{ cite book |last1=Gellman |first1=Barton |last2=English |first2=Beth |title=In the Nation's Service, Seventy-Five Years at the Woodrow Wilson School |date=2005 |isbn=0-9773544-0-7 |publisher= Woodrow Wilson School of and International Affairs }}</ref> In May after two months of preparation, "the 3-story, L-shaped, 7,000,000-pound building was moved 296 feet in 12 hours by 38 men and 9 hydraulic jacks..."<ref name="InNatServ"/> The Annex, renamed Corwin Hall, subsequently housed the department of political science and Center of International Studies.  The new structure, completed in the fall of 1965, was originally named Woodrow Wilson Hall, but was "renamed Robertson Hall in 1988 to honor its benefactors" and as President Goheen characterized upon its completion, was "a fit embodiment and expression of the high aspiration we hold for the [SPIA]."<ref name="InNatServ"/>

In approximately 2005, the SPIA established the Bernstein Gallery in Robertson Hall. The gallery "presents art exhibitions to stimulate thinking about contemporary policy issues and to enable understanding the world beyond the power of words. Each year, six curated shows are presented ... and ... are complemented by ... panel discussions with experts from Princeton University, the School and outside organizations." The exhibits are "...Integrated with the School's multidisciplinary approach ... to enhance the impact of the course curriculum and to deepen people's commitment to the ideals of public service."<ref>{{cite web |title=Bernstein Gallery |url=https://spia.princeton.edu/about/bernstein-gallery |website=spia.princeton.edu |access-date= 27 October 2020 }}</ref> A retrospective of political art was held in 2017.<ref>{{cite web |title= Bernstein Gallery Retrospective: 15 Years of Political Art |date=12 Feb 2017 |access-date= 3 March 2021 |url=https://issuu.com/woodrowwilsonschool/docs/web_retrospective |department=issu.com |publisher= Princeton School of Public and International Affairs }}</ref> The lower level lobby and gallery are named for [[Marver Bernstein]], SPIA's first dean<ref>{{cite web|title=Marver Hillel Bernstein|url=https://www.brandeis.edu/president/past/bernstein.html |access-date=1 October 2020 }}</ref> and his wife, Sheva. Bernstein participated in Robertson's architectural selection process and his wife, an interior designer, participated in its mid-century modern interior.{{citation needed|date=February 2021}}<!-- I have been told that there is a plaque inside the hall acknowledging his and his wife's input -->

In 2012,<ref>{{cite web|title=Welcome to Ai Weiwei at Princeton|url=https://www.princeton.edu/aiww/about/|website=princeton.edu}}</ref> the Princeton University Art Museum announced the installation of the "Circle of Animals/Zodiac Heads" exhibit by Chinese artist [[Ai Weiwei]]<ref>{{cite web|title=Ai Weiwei - Circle of Animals / Zodiac Heads|url=https://www.zodiacheads.com}}</ref> on Scudder Plaza.

In 2019–2020 Robertson Hall underwent a major renovation of its "offices, work areas, and gathering spaces" to provide more open, collaborative spaces. The exterior, auditorium, and lower-level classrooms were largely unchanged.<ref>{{cite web|title= Here's what happened at the last CPUC meeting of 2020
 |last=Shevin |first=Zachary |date=15 Dec 2020|access-date=29 March 2021 |url=  https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2020/12/princeton-cpuc-campus-spring-2021-construction-testing |work=Daily Princetonian }}</ref><ref>{{cite web|title= Robertson Hall Renovations Begin |url= https://paw.princeton.edu/article/robertson-hall-renovations-begin |date=6 March 2021 |access-date= 29 March 2021 |work=Princeton Alumni Weekly }}</ref>

On October 5, 2019, the university dedicated a memorial marker, named Double Sights, on the west side of the Fountain of Freedom.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |title=University dedicates marker addressing the complex legacy of Woodrow Wilson |url=https://www.princeton.edu/news/2019/10/10/university-dedicates-marker-addressing-complex-legacy-woodrow-wilson |access-date=2022-11-06 |website=Princeton University |language=en}}</ref> Designed by 2019 [[MacArthur Fellows Program|MacArthur Fellow]] and [[The Dorothy and Lillian Gish Prize|Gish Prize]] winner [[Walter J. Hood|Walter Hood]], the memorial was meant to catalyze “stimulus to reflection and an invitation to dialogue" about the controversial legacy of [[Woodrow Wilson and race|Woodrow Wilson]].<ref name=":0" />  The marker is a 39-foot statue consisting of a white column and a black column, coated in stone-like glass engraved with quotes displaying both the honorable and racist aspects of Wilson's legacy.<ref name=":0" /> The marker was the product of a recommendation by a 2015 University committee  to create a “permanent marker”  that “educates the campus community and others about the positive and negative dimensions of Wilson’s legacy.” <ref name=":0" />

The school is a full member of the [[Association of Professional Schools of International Affairs]] (APSIA), a group of schools of public policy, public administration, and international studies.{{citation needed|date=March 2020}}

==Faculty==
Nearly all full-time Princeton School faculty members have dual appointments with other departments at the university. The school also has visiting professors, lecturers, and practitioners from the world of public and international affairs that teach. Faculty members at the school include Nobel Laureates, Pulitzer Prize winners, a university president,<ref>[[Marver Bernstein|Marver H. Bernstein, first Dean of the Woodrow Wilson School and 4th president of Brandeis University]]</ref> and former ambassadors.<ref>{{cite web|title=Faculty & Research|url=http://wws.princeton.edu/faculty-research|website=wws.princeton.edu}}</ref> Nobel Laureates include [[Angus Deaton]], [[Daniel Kahneman]], [[Paul Krugman]], and [[W. Arthur Lewis|Arthur Lewis]].

==Controversy==
In July 2002, dissenting family members of the Robertson Foundation board, which was established initially by a $35 million gift in 1961, filed suit seeking to more narrowly focus the SPIA curriculum on training for careers in government and public service vs. Princeton's broader conception of "public affairs" which "embraces some non-government activities, for example, certain types of work in journalism, in private foundations, and in business, labor and [[consumer organization]]s."<ref>{{Cite web |title=Robertson Foundation lawsuit Q&A |url=https://www.princeton.edu/news/2007/03/26/robertson-foundation-lawsuit-qa |work=Princeton University News |date=26 March 2007 |access-date=30 March 2020}}</ref> A settlement was reached in 2008.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Settlement retains Princeton's control, use of Robertson funds |url=https://www.princeton.edu/news/2008/12/10/settlement-retains-princetons-control-use-robertson-funds |work=Princeton University News |date=10 December 2008 |access-date=6 September 2022}}</ref>
